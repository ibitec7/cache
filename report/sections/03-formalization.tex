\section{The quantity, formally}
\label{sec:formalization}

This section states what is estimated and under which condition it is identified.
The prose before each equation says what the equation buys; the tables define every
symbol, so the section can be read without the derivations in the appendix.

\subsection{Reuse distance and the miss-ratio curve}
\label{sec:reuse}

Fix a trace of $N$ requests and a window. For request $i$ with key $k_i$, let $p(i)$
be the previous position at which the same key was requested. The backward reuse
distance is the number of distinct keys requested strictly between the two accesses:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reuse}
  R_i \;=\; \bigl|\{\,k_j \;:\; p(i) < j < i\,\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}

with $R_i=\infty$ when no previous access exists inside the window. The distance is
a function of the past only. It is therefore observed at request $i$ whenever the
previous access is inside the window, and the end of the window does not censor it.

Under LRU, a request hits exactly when the objects touched since its previous access
still fit. At unit object size this is a threshold on the distance:

\begin{proposition}[LRU hit condition]\label{prop:lru}
For a cache of capacity $C$ objects under LRU, request $i$ hits if and only if
$R_i < C$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mrc}
  \mathrm{MRC}(C) \;=\; \Pr(R \ge C),
\end{equation}
the survival function of the backward reuse distance, and compulsory requests
($R=\infty$) miss at every finite capacity.
\end{proposition}

The proof is the stack argument of Mattson et al.\ and is repeated in
\cref{app:proofs}. The operational content is that capacity is a threshold, not a
regressor: one model of $R$ answers every $C$.

\subsection{A discrete hazard for the distance}
\label{sec:hazard}

Discretise the distance into $M$ bins with edges $e_0<\dots<e_M$, where bin $b$ is
$[e_b,e_{b+1})$. The discrete hazard is the probability that the distance falls in
bin $b$, given that it has not fallen in an earlier bin:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:hazard}
  h_b(x) \;=\; \Pr\bigl(R\in b \;\big|\; R\ge e_b,\; X=x\bigr),
\end{equation}

with $X$ the request features of \cref{sec:features}. The survival function is the
product of within-bin survival probabilities,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:survival}
  S(k\mid x) \;=\; \prod_{b=0}^{k}\bigl(1-h_b(x)\bigr),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{MRC}(C) \;=\; p_c + (1-p_c)\,\mathbb{E}_x\bigl[S(\beta(C)\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta(C)$ is the index of the bin containing $C-1$ and $p_c=\Pr(R=\infty)$ is
the compulsory mass. The first term is the requests no capacity can turn into hits;
the second is the part the model estimates. \Cref{tab:sym-hazard} fixes the
notation.

\subsection{What is observed, and where the curve stops being identified}
\label{sec:identification}

Every eligible request contributes one row per bin it survived: bins
$0,\dots,\beta(R)$, with the label set at $\beta(R)$ when $R\le e_M$ and no positive
label when the distance exceeds the ceiling. This grouped risk set is the
discrete-time likelihood written once per bin rather than once per unit of distance.
It is exact up to the assumption that the hazard is constant inside a bin.

Two boundaries follow. First, the observation ceiling: distances above $e_M$ are
right-censored at $e_M$, so capacities at or above the last real bin edge $b_{M-1}$
are not identified and the pipeline raises instead of extrapolating. Second, the
compulsory mass: as $C$ grows, $\mathrm{MRC}(C)\to p_c$, never to zero. A target hit
ratio above $1-p_c$ is unreachable on that trace, which is why
\cref{sec:experiments} states targets as a fraction of that ceiling.

\subsection{Metrics, and what failing each one means}
\label{sec:metrics}

Let $\hat m$ be the predicted curve and $m^\ast$ the exact one on capacities
$C_1,\dots,C_J$. \Cref{tab:metrics} defines the five curve metrics and the one
decision metric. All are computed on the held-out block, once.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\caption{Metrics. ``Failing it'' is the reading a reviewer should take from a bad
value, not a threshold chosen after the fact.}
\label{tab:metrics}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Definition & Failing it means \\
\midrule
MAE & $\frac1J\sum_j|\hat m_j-m^\ast_j|$ & the curve is wrong on average \\
MAX & $\max_j|\hat m_j-m^\ast_j|$ & a local blow-up, often near the knee \\
bias & $\frac1J\sum_j(\hat m_j-m^\ast_j)$ & systematic over- or under-estimation \\
MRE & mean $|\hat m_j-m^\ast_j|/m^\ast_j$ for $m^\ast_j\ge0.01$ & relative error where the cache is useful \\
ABC & $\int|\hat m-m^\ast|\,\mathrm{d}\log C \,/\,\Delta\log C$ & error averaged over decades of capacity \\
ratio & $\hat C_q/C_q$ for target hit $q$ & a wrong provisioning decision, the applied cost \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

MAE is the headline because it is the quantity a capacity decision integrates over;
MAX catches the failure MAE hides; bias distinguishes a curve that is wrong
everywhere in the same direction from one that is noisy; MRE refuses to average
relative errors where the truth is effectively zero; ABC makes two grids comparable
in log capacity. The provisioning ratio is the only metric an operator acts on
directly, and it is reported separately for that reason.
