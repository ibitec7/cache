## Section notes (what each must actually say)

**1 Hook.** Open on a real decision: a cache tier is sized in weeks, the curve that should
drive it takes a different kind of measurement, and getting it wrong costs either money or a
latency cliff. No history, no "in today's world".

**2 MRC.** Define the object: `m(C)` is the fraction of requests that miss in a fully
associative LRU cache of capacity `C`. State the identity that the whole post rests on: a
request hits exactly when its reuse distance `R` is below `C`, so `m(C) = P(R >= C)`. Show the
four measured curves (D2) and name the knee. Explain why the curve is monotone and why it
flattens at the compulsory floor `p_c`. Table T1 gives the shape per cluster.

**3 Why it matters.** Two costs: over-provisioning spends memory that another tier needs;
under-provisioning walks the latency cliff because miss ratio is not linear in capacity near
the knee. Give the measured elbow for one cluster and the capacity ratio a bad estimator
costs (T2, from `artifacts/tables/*_metrics.json` provisioning block). One sentence on
multi-tier placement: the curve is what a tiering decision compares.

**4 Related work.** Group, do not enumerate: exact (Mattson 1970 stack distance, SHARDS,
counter stacks), approximate-with-simulation (Talus, libCacheSim), distribution-free marginal
(Kaplan-Meier), and learned (hazard model here). State the axis of the comparison in one
line: memory cost of the estimator against fidelity to the exact curve. T3 is a delta table:
what each method assumes, what it costs, what it cannot do. The post must say plainly that
this is not the first learned MRC estimator and point at what is different: covariates over
per-request metadata, a discrete hazard with an explicit censoring correction, and an
evaluation on 699 held-out windows.

**5 Data.** Four clusters from the CMU mirror of the Twitter cache traces (Yang et al. 2020).
Explain what a cluster is: one production cache front-end, anonymized, 54 of them published,
each a sorted CSV of `timestamp, key, key_size, value_size, client_id, operation, ttl`.
Cluster52 is the 13.4B-request, 6.4-day shard; the other three are small shards used for
development. T4 is the distribution table (requests, unique keys, `R` p50/p99/max, cold
fraction, operation mix), generated by Task B1, not typed. One paragraph on why the four
differ so much (p99 reuse distance spans about 8 to about 1M) and what that means: the
transfer test is not a small perturbation.

**6 The statistical model.** Build it in four moves, each one paragraph plus an equation:
1. An episode: from a request to the next access of the same key. Its lifetime is the reuse
   distance. Episodes that never close are right-censored, and the post must say what that
   word means ("we know the key survived past this point, we do not know how long after").
2. Discretize into `K = 32` log-spaced buckets. The hazard is a conditional probability:
   `h_t(x) = P(d = t | d >= t, x)`, with `d` the lifetime and `x` the 12 metadata features.
   Explain why the condition is `d >= t`: at bucket `t` the episode has already survived.
3. Survival is the product: `S(t|x) = prod_{s<=t} (1 - h_s(x))`. Say why a product and not a
   sum: surviving `t` buckets means not failing in any of them, and the buckets are
   conditional, so the events chain.
4. The curve follows: `m(C) = p_c + (1 - p_c) * (1 - S(bin(C-1) | x))` averaged over requests.
   Explain the `p_c` term: a first access cannot hit at any capacity, so it is not a failure
   of the model, it is a floor the model must not try to explain.
D5 is the state machine of one episode (opens, at risk per bucket, event or censored). D6 is
the same thing as a layer stack from lifetime to curve.

Bias paragraph: the risk-set expansion and the censoring convention are what keep the hazard
unbiased. A censored episode contributes "no event" rows only up to its censoring bucket; a
naive fit that treats it as a non-event for all `K` buckets understates the hazard and bends
the curve upward, which is exactly the bias the Kaplan-Meier baseline also corrects.

**7 Why survival, not regression.** Three arguments, each with the mechanism named:
1. `m(C) = P(R >= C)` is a distributional question, so the estimand is a survival function,
   not a point prediction. A regressor on `R` predicts a mean, and the mean of a heavy tail
   with mass at `infinity` is not the object anyone wants.
2. Censoring. A regressor needs a target for the unobserved episodes; the honest options are
   to drop them (selection bias: short reuse distances are over-represented) or impute
   (invented data). A hazard model consumes them correctly.
3. Bias-variance. Ask the variance question in the right place: per request the hazard is a
   smooth function of a few features, so a high-variance learner is tolerable *because the
   risk set averages over millions of rows*. State the trade honestly: capacity is the knob
   (`num_leaves`, `min_data_in_leaf`), and the measured in-domain residual is dominated by a
   level offset, not shape noise (T14), which is the signature of a low-variance fit.

**8 The learner.** LightGBM: gradient boosted decision trees over binned features. Explain
each piece in one clause: histogram binning (features are bucketed once, so splits cost
`O(bins)` per feature, not `O(rows)`), leaf-wise growth with `num_leaves` as the capacity
knob, and GOSS as an *optional* sampling mode. **This project does not use GOSS**: the shipped
parameters are plain `gbdt`, and the post must say so rather than describe a mode we do not
run. What we do use: `objective=binary`, `num_leaves=63`, `learning_rate=0.05`,
`min_data_in_leaf=100`, `feature_fraction=0.9`, `bagging_fraction=0.8` with `bagging_freq=1`,
`lambda_l2=1.0`, `deterministic=True`, `force_row_wise=True`. Explain `force_row_wise` as the
determinism choice that makes replicate runs bit-comparable, at a small throughput cost.
Say why trees: the hazard is monotone-ish in some features and has sharp interactions in
others (gap, age, frequency), and a tree ensemble gets those without a hand-built basis.

**9 Loss, validation, class balance.** Binary logloss on the expanded (episode, bucket) table;
early stopping on validation logloss, 300 to 400 rounds. **No class weighting is applied**
(`no scale_pos_weight`, `no is_unbalance`): say why. The label is rare per row (one event
among `K` at-risk rows per episode), but the row count is what the loss averages over and the
probability itself is the quantity the curve integrates, so re-weighting would distort `S(t)`
to fix a symptom that does not exist. One caveat: the effective sample is episodes, not rows,
so the loss's implicit weighting is not the deployment weighting. State it, do not fix it.

**10 Search.** Optuna TPE versus grid (exponential in dimensions, no information sharing) and
random (information-free, but better than grid in high dimensions because it does not waste
runs on a coarse lattice). TPE models the objective's density per quantile and samples where
it expects improvement. Then the measured trap: at 12 trials three clusters returned identical
parameters and a rank correlation of exactly 1.000, because seeded TPE spends its first 10
trials on random draws; extended past trial 20 the studies diverged and every test MAE
improved (T8). Rule: a study is reported only past trial 20.

**11 Pipeline.** The honest shape: **streaming ingest, one frozen fit, no sliding window and
no expanding refit.** Say what that means: windows of 5M requests are read one at a time from
parquet so peak memory is one window plus the pooled training rows; the fit sample is drawn
uniformly from the first 60% of the trace; the booster is frozen before any test window is
opened; and each test window is scored independently into its own file. Name the two
deployment-honest details: the bin grid comes from the train block alone (later periods do
not exist at deployment time), and the score files are invalidated by mtime so a stale score
can never be reported under new parameters. D8 is the sequence.

**12 Validations.** OOT holds the trace, the grid and the distribution fixed and moves only
time. LOCO holds the code fixed and moves the distribution. Say what each unlocked: OOT gave
the deployment claim (699 of 699 windows), LOCO gave the transfer claim and its limit (about
4x MAE cost on three of four traces, with one inversion that the retrain curve explains). D9
is the swimlane of what each holds fixed.

**13 Results.** OOT table (T11), LOCO table (T12), the model-versus-KM picture as a dumbbell
(D10), the retrain curve (T13 and D11), the Optuna comparison (T8), and the baseline table
(T3b) with `single_row` marked as an oracle where it appears. Every mean carries `n`; the
cluster52 row carries its block-bootstrap interval.

**14 What the errors look like.** The finding: in-domain, `|bias|/MAE` is 0.74 to 0.97 and the
sign is always negative, so the residual is a near-constant under-prediction of 0.1 to 0.4
percentage points across all capacities, not shape error (T14). Say why that matters
practically: the failure direction is under-provisioning, and a per-trace scalar would remove
most of it. Mark the recalibration as a hypothesis that has not been run.

**15 Shipping one model.** Answer the question the reader came for. Trained elsewhere, the
model still beats the floor by 9x to 60x on every window, but the absolute error is about 4x
worse, so it is a good prior and a poor final answer. Local data closes the gap: 25M to 60M
requests for two traces, and cluster10 had not plateaued at 60M. Deployment reading: retrain
on each trace with a few tens of millions of requests and a re-tune past trial 20; expect
drift to show up as bias first, so monitor the level offset rather than the shape.

**16 What we got wrong.** Three retractions with their mechanism: the 12-trial study, the
stale score files that made three clusters identical to 16 digits, and the `single_row`
baseline that reads the truth. This section is why a reader believes section 13.

**17 Limitations.** LRU only (the curve is defined by LRU hit), object capacity only (byte
curves exist in the code but are not what the four traces report here), one corpus and one
week-to-six-days span per trace, no online adaptation, censoring convention fixed by the
observation window, and a 32-bucket discretization whose top bucket merges everything beyond
the cap. Each one names what it would take to fix.

**18 Conclusion.** Four sentences of claims we will defend, then the reproduction block:
fetch, build labels and truth, fit, score, reduce. Six commands, and the exact paths.

# Part D: grounding corrections (do not undo these)

These are the facts the code and artifacts establish, against the brief's assumptions. Each
one is a place where the blog would otherwise state something false and a reader would catch
it.

1. **No GOSS.** `DEFAULT_PARAMS` (`src/mrc/pipeline.py:53`) sets no `boosting` key, so the
   learner is plain gradient boosting. The post explains GOSS as a LightGBM feature and says
   plainly that this project does not use it. GOSS is also not obviously desirable here:
   down-sampling large gradients changes the effective label distribution that `S(t)` is read
   from.
2. **Classification, not regression.** `objective="binary"`, `metric="binary_logloss"`,
   early stopping on validation logloss. The estimand is a probability per (episode, bucket).
3. **No class weighting.** No `scale_pos_weight`, no `is_unbalance`. Section 9 must justify
   the absence instead of implying a weighting exists.
4. **One frozen fit, not a sliding or expanding window.** `prepare_windows`
   (`src/mrc/pipeline.py:352`) streams windows and draws one uniform sample from the train
   block; the model is fit once and never refit per window. The retrain curve varies only the
   budget.
5. **`single_row` is an oracle.** It reads `eval_rtilde` for the block it scores and reports
   MAE 0.000000 by construction. It appears only as a labelled oracle in T3b.
6. **`n` is not decoration.** Cluster52 has 672 OOT windows, the other traces have 7, 8, 12.
   A mean over 7 and a mean over 672 are different evidence and the tables say so.
7. **The 12-trial Optuna numbers are retracted**, not superseded silently.
